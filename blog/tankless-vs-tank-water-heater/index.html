    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in South Dakota

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    Two Ways to Heat Water

    A tank heater keeps 40 to 80 gallons hot around the clock. A tankless unit heats water only while a tap is open. Both can be gas or electric, both can serve a whole house, and the choice usually comes down to how your family uses hot water and what your gas line and water will support. Neither is the right answer everywhere.

    The Tank Heater

    Tanks are simple and cheap to replace. A standard 50-gallon gas tank swap is usually a same-day job. The tradeoff is standby loss, since the burner or elements cycle all day to keep stored water hot whether anyone is home or not, and the tank holds a fixed amount, so the fourth shower in a row runs cold. Most tanks last somewhere between 8 and 12 years.

    Tanks also forgive a lot. A small gas line or some scale buildup rarely bothers them. If the power goes out, a gas tank with a standing pilot keeps making hot water, and the stored 50 gallons can even serve as an emergency supply drawn from the drain valve. That counts for something during a storm.

    Housing in South Dakota mixes older farmhouses and town homes with newer subdivisions, so plumbing can range from cast iron and galvanized to modern PEX.

    The Tankless Heater

    Tankless heaters are rated by flow, in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. A large gas unit might deliver 8 or 9 gallons per minute when incoming water is mild. Colder incoming water means a bigger temperature rise, so the same unit delivers less flow in winter, and two showers plus a dishwasher can push a small unit past what it can keep up with. Sizing matters more than brand.

    The upside is real. Endless hot water is the headline, as long as the unit is sized for your peak demand. Without standby loss, a gas tankless unit typically burns noticeably less fuel than a tank, it hangs on a wall instead of eating closet floor space, and a well-kept unit can run 20 years. The heat exchanger is the part to protect.

    Maintenance looks different on each. Drain a few gallons from a tank every year to pull out the sediment. The anode rod, a sacrificial magnesium or aluminum rod that keeps the steel from rusting, should be checked every three to five years, and replacing a spent anode is one of the cheapest ways to get extra years out of a tank. Tankless units need descaling and an inlet screen cleaning.

    What Changes the Math in a South Dakota Home

    Hard water is the norm across much of South Dakota, and many groundwater sources carry dissolved minerals like calcium, magnesium, sulfate and iron.

    Installation is where tankless costs climb. Expect a larger gas line, often 3/4-inch, plus new venting rated for the unit. Electric tankless models for a whole house can need 120 amps or more on dedicated breakers, which many older panels simply do not have room for, so a panel upgrade sometimes rides along with the job. We look at the panel and the gas meter first.

    Hard water is the other big variable. Scale coats a tankless heat exchanger quickly and trips error codes. On hard water, plan to descale the unit every year with a small pump and a descaling solution circulated through the isolation valves, or put a softener ahead of it. A tank collects the same minerals as sediment on the bottom.

    Long, cold winters and hot summers put South Dakota plumbing through wide temperature swings that expand and contract pipes and fittings every year.

    Family size and habits decide the rest. Two people seldom need more than one hot tap at a time. A family of five with teenagers and a soaking tub is a different load entirely, and that house needs either a large tankless unit sized for three fixtures at once or a bigger tank with a fast recovery rate. The first question is how many showers run together.

    Code details matter on either type. Every tank gets a T&P relief valve with a discharge line run down to within a few inches of the floor or to a drain. Gas units of both kinds need proper combustion air and venting, and a tankless unit needs isolation valves so it can be flushed without cutting into the plumbing every year. Inspectors check every one of those.

    Ask about recovery rate when comparing tanks. A gas tank reheats a full load much faster than an electric one. First-hour rating, printed on the yellow energy label, tells you how many gallons a tank delivers in the first hour of heavy use, and it is a better number to compare than tank size alone for a busy house. Hybrid heat pump tanks are another option.

    When to Call a Professional

    A replacement is the cheapest time to switch. A tank past 10 years or weeping at the base is worth pricing both ways now.
