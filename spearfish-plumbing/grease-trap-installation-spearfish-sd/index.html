    What Goes Into a Grease Trap Installation in Spearfish, SD

    Commercial Kitchen Grease Trap Installs in Spearfish

    Every commercial install starts with a fixture list. The tech walks the kitchen and writes down every fixture that drains to the trap and how fast each one empties. From there we pick a hydromechanical unit or a gravity tank and figure out where it can sit with a straight shot to the building sewer. Fewer bends, fewer clogs.

    Hydro-Mechanical Grease Trap Installation in Spearfish, SD

    Hydromechanical traps use baffles and a vented flow control to pull grease out of water moving at a set rate, usually 20 to 100 gallons per minute. You can put one under a sink, beside a dish station or in a pit in the slab. The flow control is not optional. Without it, a dumped sink pushes water through faster than the unit is rated for and the grease goes out the outlet. We look for units certified to PDI G-101 or ASME A112.14.3.

    Grease Trap Installation for Schools and Institutions in Spearfish

    School cafeterias and hospital kitchens cook in big batches and wash in big batches, which means large pot sinks and a dish machine running hard for two hours after each meal. Most end up with an outdoor gravity interceptor. We schedule around the calendar. School jobs fit into breaks, and hospital jobs get phased so meals keep going out.

    Emergency Grease Trap Services in Spearfish, SD

    A grease trap backing up into the floor sinks at the start of Friday dinner service means a closed kitchen and a health inspector who may not let you reopen until it is fixed. First job is figuring out whether the clog is in the trap or the pipe on either side of it. Often it is the outlet. A failed trap can often get a stopgap unit that night and a proper replacement later in the week.

    Replacement and Upgrade Services in Spearfish, SD

    Steel traps from decades back rust through at the baffle seams, and by then the outlet is usually passing grease straight to the sewer. A camera down the outlet line tells us how much damage the old unit already did. Sometimes the line needs jetting too. Upgrading also gives you a chance to move from a 20 GPM trap to a 35 or 50 if the menu or seat count grew.

    Grease Trap Sizing and Placement in Spearfish, SD

    Sizing starts with fixture drain rates and ends with where the trap can physically go. Long runs to the trap let grease cool and coat the line, so we keep the trap as close to the fixtures as we can. Closer is better. We also check that a pump truck can reach an outdoor tank with about 100 feet of hose, since a tank the hauler cannot reach costs you more on every service.

    Zurn Grease Trap Installation in Spearfish

    Zurn's indoor grease interceptors cover the common sizes from small under-sink units up to 100 GPM, and we set enough of them to know where the gaskets give trouble. After a few years of hot water and scraping, the lid gasket is what usually leaks. We keep spares on the truck. We mount the supplied flow control on the inlet, at the height the instructions list, and vent it.

    Gravity Grease Interceptor Installation in Spearfish, SD

    Concrete or polyethylene, a buried gravity interceptor is a two-compartment tank with tees on the inlet and outlet that reach down below the grease layer. Precast concrete weighs more and handles a parking lot better than plastic. Poly is easier to set. With a plastic tank in wet ground we add anti-flotation anchoring, because an empty tank in saturated soil can pop right out of the hole.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Spearfish

    Installed prices for small indoor hydromechanical traps typically fall between $1,500 and $5,000. Outdoor tanks cost more, mostly for digging and putting the site back. Brand is a small part of it. We give you a written estimate after we walk the site, and it does not change unless we hit something underground nobody could see.

    We start with a fixture list and the drain rate of each fixture. Sinks are calculated by compartment volume and drain-down time, typically one or two minutes, and dish machines by the manufacturer's discharge rating. That gives the GPM figure. Hydromechanical traps are picked by that GPM rating, while gravity interceptors are sized by volume and retention time from the same data.

    Size comes from flow, and flow comes from your fixtures. We measure each connected fixture and calculate the gallons per minute they can send at once. Gravity tanks use volume instead. Outdoor tanks get sized by the sewer authority's formula, which we run for you.

    Almost always, yes. You will usually need a plumbing permit plus a sign-off from the sewer authority's pretreatment program. We pull the permit, submit the sizing calculation and schedule the rough and final inspections. Expect an inspector on site.

    Zurn and Watts make up a lot of our indoor installs, with Schier and Endura when a plastic body makes more sense. Outdoor interceptors are usually precast concrete, or polyethylene where a crane cannot reach. Any certified brand works. We skip uncertified units, since inspectors turn them down.

    In everyday talk the two names get swapped. Strictly, a grease trap is the small indoor unit rated in gallons per minute, and an interceptor is the large tank rated in gallons of capacity. Codes vary on the wording. Many codes say hydromechanical interceptor and gravity interceptor instead, and we match the local wording on the permit.

    Two warranties apply here. Manufacturer coverage on the unit varies by brand and material, and plastic bodies usually get the longer terms. We cover our workmanship, meaning leaks at our joints and settling of a tank we set, and we give you the terms in writing before we start.

    Watch the pump-out bills. Rising service frequency means the trap has been outgrown or the baffles have failed. Smell is another clue. Slow drains near the trap and failed inspections point to replacement too.
