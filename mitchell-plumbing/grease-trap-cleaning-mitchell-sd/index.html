    Large Outdoor Grease Interceptor Cleaning in Mitchell

    Gravity interceptors in the parking lot often hold 750 to 2,000 gallons across two or three compartments. We pump each compartment on its own. A hard grease cap gets broken up with a pressure wand before pumping, otherwise it plugs the suction hose and half of it stays in the tank. No one goes inside without confined-space gear.

    Outdoor Grease Interceptor Pumping in Mitchell, SD

    In-ground interceptors need a vacuum truck and usually a pressure wand to break up the cap. We open every compartment and pump each one to the floor. While the tank is empty, we look at the inlet and outlet tees, the baffle wall, the risers and the lid seals, because a cracked riser lets groundwater and dirt in. Lids go back on tight.

    Grease Line Jetting in Mitchell

    Grease that slips past a full trap coats the 4-inch line out to the main. It builds up in rings, thickest at the bottom of the pipe. A snake cuts a narrow path through and the line plugs again within weeks, while a jetter with a rotating nozzle peels the grease off the whole pipe wall. We run a camera after to confirm.

    Residential Grease Trap Cleaning in Mitchell, SD

    Homes rarely have a grease trap, though a few large custom kitchens and some home-based catering setups in Mitchell, SD put a small unit under the sink. These are usually 10 to 20 gallon units. Service means scooping out grease and solids by hand, cleaning the baffle, and seating the gasket so the lid seals tight. About thirty minutes.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Mitchell

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. The biggest variable is the gallons hauled, because disposal facilities charge by volume and a 1,500-gallon tank is a full truckload.

    Slow sinks come first. Gurgling floor drains and a sour smell by the dish station usually follow. By the time gray water is coming up through a floor sink, the trap is usually well past 25 percent full and grease is already heading down the line.

    FOG stands for fats, oils and grease. It pours as a liquid and sets up hard once it cools. In a sewer pipe it sticks to the walls in layers, catches food scraps and wipes, and eventually closes the pipe down to a pencil-sized opening.

    Short term, slow drains and smell. Longer term, a hardened grease cap that can take hours to break up. The hydrogen sulfide from rotting grease eats concrete too, and a neglected interceptor can need new baffles and risers years early.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Beyond that level, water passes through too quickly to separate, and grease leaves through the outlet even though the trap seems fine.

    There is no parts warranty on a pump-out, since nothing gets installed. We do guarantee the work itself. If the trap is not flowing right after we leave, or a backup turns out to be from something we missed, we come back and make it right.

    No permit is needed just to have a trap pumped. You do need to keep the records. Installing a new trap or interceptor, or changing the size of one, usually needs a plumbing permit and sometimes a pretreatment program review before the work starts.
