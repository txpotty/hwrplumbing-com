    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Installation in Box Elder

    Small under-sink hydromechanical traps usually land between $1,000 and $3,000 installed, and buried interceptors with excavation commonly run $5,000 to $20,000 or more. The spread comes from access and how much concrete or paving we cut. Permits add a little. We quote in writing after a site visit, with the trap, the labor and the permit fees listed as separate lines.

    For indoor traps we lean on Zurn, Watts, Schier and Endura, because their parts are easy to find and their units carry PDI or ASME certification. We like the plastic bodies from Schier where steel would rust out. Brand matters less than sizing. A well-known trap installed too small will still fail inspection, so we pick the size first and the brand second.

    As close to the grease-producing fixtures as the space allows, so grease does not cool and coat the pipe before it reaches the trap. Indoor units need room above for the lid to come off and for someone to kneel and clean. Never under a cook line. Outdoor interceptors go where a pump truck can park within reach of the hose and where the lids will not end up under a dumpster or a parked car.

    You get the manufacturer's warranty on the unit and a written labor warranty from us on the installation. Factory terms vary, so we hand you the registration card or file it for you. Keep your cleaning logs. Some manufacturers ask for proof of regular service before they honor a claim on a cracked body or a failed baffle.

    An under-sink hydromechanical trap with good access is usually a half-day job, including the flow control and a leak test. Floor-recessed units take longer because we cut and patch concrete. Outdoor tanks are different. Plan on two to five days for a gravity interceptor once the permit is in hand, depending on depth, soil and how much paving we have to restore.

    From first call to final inspection, most indoor jobs take one to two weeks, and most of that is waiting on the permit and the part. Our time on site is usually one day. Outdoor jobs run longer. Gravity interceptors can take three to six weeks end to end because of plan review and tank delivery.

    The common rule is to clean before grease and solids fill 25 percent of the trap's depth. Busy kitchens with small indoor traps often hit that mark every week. Outdoor tanks go longer. Many gravity interceptors are pumped every 30 to 90 days, and your local FOG program may set a maximum interval no matter what the trap looks like.

    Indoor hydromechanical traps and outdoor gravity interceptors cover nearly every job we do. For fry-heavy kitchens we also set automatic units that skim grease on a timer. Most cafes go hydromechanical. Big-volume kitchens and anything the local FOG program flags as high risk usually end up with a gravity interceptor outside the building.
